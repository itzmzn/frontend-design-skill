# Commerce & Retail

Commerce design should make products desirable without making purchasing confusing. Product imagery, price, variant selection, availability, trust, and the next purchase action must remain easy to understand.

## Use this reference for

Furniture and interiors, food and drink, beauty and fragrance, watches and jewellery, books and publishing, apparel and sport, hardware and instruments, automotive products, marketplaces, and direct-to-consumer stores.

## Storefront structure

Lead with one campaign or collection rather than several competing promotions. Keep search, category access, account/cart state, and the shopping bag reachable from the first navigation layer. A homepage usually benefits from a focused hero, one primary collection, one supporting story or collection, and a restrained footer rather than a long stack of deal modules.

Product grids should use consistent image ratios, field order, spacing, and price alignment. On desktop, three or four columns often provide a useful balance between visual scale and browsing density; collapse deliberately for tablet and mobile rather than squeezing cards.

## Product cards

Let imagery carry most of the card's visual weight. Follow with product or brand context, a clear name, price, and only the information needed to make a browsing decision. Avoid badge clusters. If a status such as “Limited run” or “Out of stock” matters, present it once and quietly.

Hover can reveal a second image or quick action on pointer devices, but essential information and purchase access must not depend on hover. Adding an item must produce immediate, visible feedback such as an updated cart count, inline confirmation, or cart drawer.

## Product detail and purchase flow

Keep title, price, selected variant, availability, quantity, and primary purchase action together as one coherent purchase module. Large screens can pair a media gallery with a sticky purchase column; mobile can use a carefully designed sticky buy action if it does not cover content.

Variant selection must clearly communicate the active option and unavailable combinations. Product imagery, price, stock, SKU, or other dependent information should update consistently when a variant changes.

Cart and checkout should show itemized products, quantities, subtotal, delivery/handling costs, discounts, taxes where applicable, and the final amount before commitment. Forms should request only necessary information and show validation near the affected field.

## Promotion and trust

Use promotions with restraint. One compact announcement surface is usually enough. Avoid immediate entry popups, spinning wheels, stacked countdowns, and aggressive discount interruptions unless the product explicitly requires that style.

Display delivery expectations, returns, payment terms, and order status where they affect purchase confidence. If cash on delivery or another special payment method is supported, explain the amount and verification requirements clearly.

Post-purchase views should confirm the order, summarize what was purchased, and communicate the next fulfillment state.

## Visual direction

Luxury and boutique retail can pair editorial serif display type with restrained body typography and warm neutrals. Streetwear can use stronger grotesque display faces and a single forceful accent. Industrial retail benefits from precise grids, technical specifications, and tabular numeric treatment. In every case, the product should remain the visual protagonist.

## Image briefs

When imagery must be generated, specify the actual product, material, surface, lighting, background, camera distance, and required aspect ratio. For example: a skincare bottle on travertine in soft morning light; a streetwear editorial against concrete with directional flash; or a sculptural chair in a sunlit architectural interior. Generate coherent sets rather than unrelated hero and card imagery.

## Avoid

Avoid crowded flash-sale aesthetics unless intentionally requested, inconsistent product ratios, hidden prices, purchase controls that fail to show feedback, and decorative badges that compete with the product. Never let a sticky cart or buy bar cover essential mobile content.
